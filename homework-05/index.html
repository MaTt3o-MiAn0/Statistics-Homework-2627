Welford's recurrences and sampling estimators

A single-pass, numerically stable way to compute mean and variance, used to check how sample means and sample variances estimate μ and σ².

Summary

Exercise 1 derives Welford's recurrences for the sum of squares and the variance, in the same way as Knuth's recurrence for the mean, and implements them in three lines of code. Exercise 2 gives each of m hackers a trajectory of n scores between 0 and 10, computes each hacker's sampling mean and sampling variance with Welford's method, and compares their averages with the theoretical μ and σ².

The averages agree with μ and σ², because the sample mean and the sample variance with denominator n − 1 are unbiased estimators. Dividing by n instead underestimates σ² by the factor (n − 1)/n. As n grows, both estimators vary less from hacker to hacker, with a standard deviation that falls like 1/√n: this is what makes them useful for inference.

Ex. 1: Welford's recurrence formulas

For data x1, x2, … let Mk be the mean of the first k values and SSk = Σi≤k (xi − Mk)² their sum of squared deviations. The goal is to update both when a new value arrives, without storing the data and without summing large squares.

Step 1

Knuth's recurrence for the mean

k·Mk = (k − 1)·Mk−1 + xk, so dividing by k:

Mk = Mk−1 + (xk − Mk−1) / k

Write δ = xk − Mk−1. Then Mk − Mk−1 = δ/k and xk − Mk = δ(k − 1)/k.

Step 2

Welford's recurrence for SS

Split SSk into the first k − 1 terms and the last one, and write xi − Mk = (xi − Mk−1) − δ/k:

SSk = SSk−1 − (2δ/k)·Σi<k(xi − Mk−1) + (k − 1)δ²/k² + δ²(k − 1)²/k².

The middle sum is 0 (deviations from a mean add up to zero), and the last two terms add up to δ²(k − 1)/k = δ·(xk − Mk):

SSk = SSk−1 + (xk − Mk−1)(xk − Mk)

Step 3

Recurrence for the variance

The sample variance is s²k = SSk/(k − 1). Using SSk−1 = (k − 2)·s²k−1, for k ≥ 2:

s²k = (k − 2)/(k − 1) · s²k−1 + δ²/k

The start is M1 = x1, SS1 = 0. The biased variance SSk/k follows in the same way.

Code

The core logic in three lines

count += 1;
const delta = x - mean;           // δ with the old mean
mean += delta / count;            // Knuth
sumOfSquares += delta * (x - mean); // Welford

The last three lines are the core; then s² = sumOfSquares/(count − 1). The update needs O(1) memory and one pass over the data, so it works on a stream of scores.

Why not Σx² − n·x̄²

Numerical stability

The textbook one-pass formula subtracts two large, nearly equal numbers. In double precision the difference loses its significant digits (catastrophic cancellation). Welford only accumulates deviations from the current mean, which stay small. Try the demo with the offset: for 4, 7, 13, 16 plus 10⁹ the textbook formula gives −170.67 (a negative variance), Welford gives the exact 30.

    Ex. 2: the experiment

    Each of m hackers attempts n systems and receives one score in [0, 10] per system, drawn independently from a fixed distribution with mean μ and variance σ². For each hacker, Welford's recurrences give the sampling mean x̄ and the sampling variance s² of the n scores, in a single pass.

    What is compared

    For hacker j: x̄j = Mn and s²j = SSn/(n − 1) from Welford.

    Across the m hackers: the average of the x̄j against μ, and the average of the s²j against σ².

    Theory: E[x̄] = μ and Var(x̄) = σ²/n; E[s²] = σ² and Var(s²) = (μ₄ − σ⁴(n − 3)/(n − 1))/n, where μ₄ is the fourth central moment.

    Conclusion

    In the default run the average of the sampling means is 3.0001 against μ = 3, and the average of the sampling variances is 2.089 against σ² = 2.1. The differences are within the precision of the check itself: an average over m = 2000 hackers has a standard error of about 0.003 for the means and 0.006 for the variances.

    Unbiasedness

    x̄ and s² are unbiased: on average they hit μ and σ². The variance with denominator n is biased: its average is 2.068, close to σ²(n − 1)/n = 2.079. The n − 1 (Bessel's correction) compensates for measuring deviations from x̄ instead of from the unknown μ.

    Why it matters for inference

    In practice there is only one sample. Unbiasedness guarantees that the estimate has no systematic error, and the spread σ/√n (estimated by s/√n, the standard error) gives its precision. Together with the approximately normal shape of x̄ (central limit theorem) this is the basis of confidence intervals and tests, such as x̄ ± 1.96·s/√n for μ.

    Effect of the number of systems

    The experiment is repeated for n = 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500 and 1000 systems per hacker, with the same number of hackers.

    Bias

    Average estimate divided by its target

    The sampling mean and s² stay at 1 for every n (within 0.3% for the means and about 1% for the variances from n = 10; at n = 2 the variance check itself is noisy). The biased variance follows (n − 1)/n: 0.52 at n = 2, 0.90 at n = 10, 0.98 at n = 50.

    Variability

    Standard deviation across hackers, both axes logarithmic

    Both lines have slope −1/2. The sampling means go from 1.01 at n = 2 to 0.046 at n = 1000, matching σ/√n. The sampling variances go from 2.99 to 0.092, matching √Var(s²), which behaves like √((μ₄ − σ⁴)/n) for large n.

    Increasing the number of systems per hacker does not change where the estimators are centered, but it makes them more concentrated: 100 times more systems give a spread 10 times smaller. For small n the sampling variance is also strongly skewed to the right (it can be much larger than σ², but not below 0), and its histogram becomes symmetric and close to normal only for larger n.

    Conclusions

    Welford's recurrences compute the mean and the variance in a single pass with constant memory, and they avoid the cancellation that makes the textbook formula fail on large values. Applied to m hackers and n systems, they show that the sampling mean and the sampling variance (with n − 1) are unbiased estimators of μ and σ², while dividing by n underestimates σ². Their variability across hackers falls like 1/√n, so a larger number of systems gives more precise estimates. This is why a single sample can be used to estimate a population parameter and to quantify the uncertainty of that estimate.
