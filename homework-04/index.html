Penetration scores, LLN and CLT

Simulating n systems and m attackers with i.i.d. increments, and studying total, average and normalized scores.

Summary

Each of m attackers goes through n systems and collects an i.i.d. score at every step. The same random walk is studied with two score models (Bernoulli and Rademacher) and three scalings: the total score Sn, the average Sn/n and the normalized score Sn/√n.

The scaling decides the behavior. The total spreads out (variance nσ²), the average concentrates on the mean (law of large numbers, variance σ²/n), and only the normalized score keeps a stable, non-trivial shape (variance σ²): in the Rademacher case it converges to the standard normal law (central limit theorem), while in the Bernoulli case it drifts unless it is centered. The number of attackers does not change one attacker: it sets how reliable the empirical distributions are.

The model

There are n systems and m attackers. Attacker j goes through the systems in order and obtains the score Xi(j) at step i. The increments are independent and identically distributed with mean μ and variance σ², and different attackers are independent: the m attackers are m independent sample trajectories.

Score models

Model 1

Bernoulli

X ∈ {0, 1}, P(X = 1) = p

The attacker penetrates system i with probability p, so Sk counts the systems penetrated so far.

μ = p, σ² = p(1 − p)

Model 2

Rademacher

X ∈ {−1, +1}, ½ each

A symmetric gain or loss of foothold at each system, with no drift: the zero-mean case.

μ = 0, σ² = 1

Scores and what theory predicts

Quantity 1

Total score

Sₙ = X₁ + … + Xₙ

Mean nμ, variance nσ²: Bernoulli np and np(1 − p), Rademacher 0 and n.

The spread grows like √n and there is no limit.

Quantity 2

Average score

Sₙ / n

Mean μ, variance σ²/n: Bernoulli p and p(1 − p)/n, Rademacher 0 and 1/n.

Law of large numbers: Sₙ/n → μ in probability. Chebyshev bounds P(|Sₙ/n − μ| > ε) by σ²/(nε²), and Hoeffding (steps of range b − a) by 2·exp(−2nε²/(b − a)²).

Quantity 3

Normalized score

Sₙ / √n

Mean μ√n, variance σ²: Bernoulli p√n and p(1 − p), Rademacher 0 and 1.

Central limit theorem: (Sₙ − nμ)/(σ√n) → N(0, 1), so for Rademacher Sₙ/√n → N(0, 1). For Bernoulli with p > 0 the mean p√n grows without bound: Sₙ/√n drifts to +∞ and only the centered version converges.

Speed depends on σ²

The same probability (about 0.11) needs n = 200 for Bernoulli with p = 0.3 and n = 1000 for Rademacher. The ratio of 5 is close to σ²Rad/σ²Bern = 1/0.21 ≈ 4.8, as the Chebyshev bound σ²/(nε²) suggests.

The bounds

Both are valid but conservative. Chebyshev falls only like 1/n. Hoeffding falls exponentially and is much closer for large n, but it is larger than 1 for small n and so is capped at 1.

Central limit theorem

In the Rademacher model μ = 0 and σ = 1, so the theorem says that Sn/√n converges in distribution to N(0, 1).

In the Bernoulli model Sn/√n drifts, so the theorem is studied for the standardized score (Sn − np)/√(np(1 − p)), which converges to N(0, 1).

The distance from the normal law is the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic Dn = supx |Fn(x) − Φ(x)|, where Fn is the empirical distribution function. Ten times as many attackers as m are used, so that the sampling noise is small.

Rate 1/√n

Sn has the parity of n, so its distribution function has jumps while Φ is continuous. At the center the jump is about 0.8/√n, so Dn cannot go below about 0.399/√n (amber line). For n ≤ 200, Dn·√n stays between 0.34 and 0.44: the 1/√n rate of the Berry–Esseen bound is reached.

Sn takes integer values, so its distribution function has jumps while Φ is continuous. The largest jump is about 1/(σ√(2πn)), so Dn cannot go below about 0.435/√n for p = 0.3 (amber line). For n ≤ 200, Dn·√n stays between 0.44 and 0.50, the same 1/√n rate, slightly higher than for Rademacher because the Bernoulli law is skewed (the Berry–Esseen constant ρ/σ³ is 1.27 instead of 1).

Reading the test

At n = 1000 the distance (0.017) is above the 5% critical value of the test (0.0096), so exact normality would be rejected. This is only because Sn/√n is discrete, not a failure of the theorem, which states convergence and not equality.

At n = 1000 the distance (0.021) is above the 5% critical value of the test (0.0096), so exact normality would be rejected. As in the Rademacher case this is a lattice effect, not a failure of the theorem.

Standardization

For Rademacher, μ = 0 and σ = 1, so Sn/√n is already standardized. For Bernoulli the score must be centered and rescaled: (Sn − np)/√(np(1 − p)) → N(0, 1) (De Moivre–Laplace). The raw Sn/√n has mean p√n and drifts.

Systems and attackers

Number of systems n

For one attacker the expected number of penetrations is np (Bernoulli) and the fluctuation grows like √n, but the relative fluctuation √((1 − p)/(pn)) shrinks: the fraction penetrated becomes predictable. For Rademacher the net score has no drift and its typical size grows like √n.

Number of attackers m

m does not change one attacker. It sharpens the estimate of the distribution and sets the fluctuation of the aggregate score T = Σj Sn(j), whose average per system and attacker, T/(mn), has standard deviation σ/√(mn).

Limits of the model

Independence and equal distribution are assumptions. If attackers share tools and information they are positively correlated, and adding attackers cannot reduce the fluctuation below the part they have in common.

Conclusions

The total score has variance nσ² and spreads out, the average has variance σ²/n and converges in probability to μ at a speed set by σ², and the normalized score has the constant variance σ². In the zero-mean Rademacher case the normalized score converges to N(0, 1) at the 1/√n rate; in the Bernoulli case the nonzero mean makes it drift, and only the centered version converges.

For n systems and m attackers: a larger system makes the fraction of penetrated systems predictable while the absolute count fluctuates more, and more attackers sharpen the aggregate estimate like 1/√m, provided they are independent.
